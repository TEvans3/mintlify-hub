The DevRel persona — what you're actually dealing with

Head of DevRel and Developer Experience is your most common first contact at Mintlify — and your most dangerous comfort zone. They feel the documentation pain personally. They love the product immediately. They take every meeting enthusiastically. And they frequently have zero budget authority and limited internal political capital.

The DevRel champion is only valuable if they have a line to the VP of Engineering or CTO and are willing to use it on your behalf. Your entire champion strategy with this persona is about qualifying that line and activating it — not deepening the relationship with someone who can't move the deal.

Mobilizer vs Talker — the DevRel edition
🟢 Mobilizer signals
Proactively names the VP Eng or CTO without you asking
Asks questions about pricing and procurement process
Follows through on small asks within 48 hours
Connects you to another stakeholder unprompted
Uses "we" when describing the problem — not "I"
Asks what they need to bring to leadership to make the case
Volunteers internal context: budget cycles, org dynamics, competing priorities
🔴 Talker signals
Takes every meeting but never advances the deal
Describes the VP Eng as "hard to reach" or "really busy"
Uses "I" when describing the problem — team pain stays abstract
Has a reason why each ask doesn't quite work right now
Enthusiastic on calls, unresponsive between them
Has never mentioned budget, doesn't ask about it
Cannot tell you what the VP Eng cares about most right now
The most expensive mistake in this deal type: spending 6 weeks building a relationship with a Head of DevRel who loves Mintlify, cannot access the VP Eng budget, and will eventually say "I tried but it's not a priority right now." That is a Talker outcome. Identify it in week 2, not week 6.
The one diagnostic question
Ask this in your second conversation — after rapport, before deep investment
"I want to make sure I'm being useful to you here — not just taking your time. If this ended up being the right solution, what would it take for it to actually get done? Like, who else would need to be involved, and is that a conversation you'd be comfortable driving?"
What you're listening for:
Mobilizer answer
Names a specific person. Describes how they'd approach the conversation. Asks what they'd need to make the case. Takes ownership of the internal motion.
Talker answer
Vague about who decides. Says "we'd have to see" or "it depends on budget cycles." Describes the internal process as complicated without offering to navigate it.
These are escalating asks — each costs the champion more than the last. Run them in order. If they pass Ask 1, move to Ask 2. If they fail any ask, you have a Talker. The ask is the test. Their response is the grade.

Ask 1 — The information ask
Week 1–2

Costs them nothing but time. A Mobilizer does it immediately. A Talker has reasons why it's complicated.

"Before our next call, would it be helpful if I put together a one-pager on how Coinbase solved this exact problem — something you could share internally if the conversation came up? And could you tell me: who else at [company] would have a perspective on this? I want to make sure I'm not creating work for you by missing someone obvious."
What the ask actually tests:

Will they name other stakeholders? Can they describe who else would care? A Mobilizer knows their internal landscape and shares it freely. A Talker keeps the relationship bilateral — just you and them — because expanding it feels risky or they genuinely don't have the access.

They pass — Mobilizer
Names the VP Eng or a specific team. Asks a clarifying question about what the one-pager should emphasize. Responds within 48 hours.
They fail — Talker
Says "it's really just me on this right now." Takes a week to respond. Gives a name but adds "I'm not sure they'd be the right person to involve yet."
If they fail Ask 1 — what to say:
"Totally understand. Help me think through this though — if you were to bring this to your VP Eng, what would you want to have ready before that conversation? I want to make sure if the timing ever is right, you're set up to make the case easily."

This reframes Ask 1 failure as a timing issue rather than a dead end. It also reveals whether they have any intention of ever having the internal conversation — or whether they're managing you away from it.

Ask 2 — The internal action ask
Week 2–3

Costs them some internal capital. They have to do something inside their org on your behalf. A Mobilizer sees this as natural. A Talker finds a reason it's not quite the right moment.

"Based on everything we've talked about — would you be comfortable sharing that one-pager with your VP Eng this week? Not as a formal proposal, just a 'hey, I've been looking at this, thought you'd find it interesting.' I can make it easy for you — give you a one-sentence context to put on it."
Why this framing works without feeling pushy:

You're not asking them to pitch Mintlify. You're asking them to share something interesting with their boss — which is something they should be doing anyway as the person responsible for developer experience. The one-sentence framing offer reduces the effort to near-zero. A Mobilizer has no reason to say no. A Talker will find one.

They pass — Mobilizer
Says yes. Follows up with what the VP's reaction was. Asks if there's more content they can share. Reports back within a week.
They fail — Talker
"Let me wait until the timing is better." Agrees but never confirms they did it. You follow up and get a vague response about the VP being busy.
If they fail Ask 2 — the direct conversation:
"I want to be straight with you — I'm trying to figure out the right way to be helpful here without wasting your time or mine. If sharing this isn't the right move right now, that's completely fine. But can you help me understand what would need to be true for this to actually move forward internally? Because I want to make sure I'm investing my time in the right way for both of us."

This is the non-pushy version of calling out the dynamic. You're not accusing them of being a Talker — you're giving them a chance to either step up or be honest that they can't move this forward. Both are useful information.

Ask 3 — The EB access ask
Week 3–4 · The definitive test

This costs them real internal capital. Only a true Mobilizer makes this happen. If they can't or won't after passing Asks 1 and 2, you have a ceiling on this deal.

"At this point I think we both know this is the right solution for [company]. The thing I keep running into is that I need to have a 20-minute conversation with your VP Eng to understand how this fits into their engineering priorities — not to pitch them, but to make sure the business case I'm building actually maps to what they care about. Would you be comfortable making that introduction? Even a quick Slack message from you saying 'I've been exploring this, I think it's worth 20 minutes' is all it takes."
Why this is the real test:

Introducing a vendor to your VP Eng is a reputational act. The champion is putting their name on you. A Mobilizer does this when they believe in the outcome — because their credibility goes up if the solution delivers. A Talker won't risk it because they're not confident enough in the outcome, or they don't actually have the relationship with the EB that they've implied.

They pass — confirmed Mobilizer
Makes the introduction. May prep you in advance on what the VP cares about. Asks to be included in the meeting. Follows up afterward to debrief.
They fail — confirmed Talker
"Let me see if I can find the right moment." Suggests you reach out to the VP directly (abdicating). Goes quiet for a week. Returns with "my VP said to go through procurement."
If they fail Ask 3 — your three options:
A
Go around them — carefully
Reach the VP Eng directly via LinkedIn with a commercial insight cold outreach. Do not mention your champion contact. Frame it as fresh outreach to a new contact.
B
Find a second thread inside the account
Identify a Senior Engineer, Technical Writer, or Engineering Manager with a different path to the VP Eng. Multi-thread before declaring the deal dead.
C
Reclassify and deprioritize
Move to Nurture in Notion. Set a 60-day revisit. Stop spending active selling time on an account with a Talker and no EB access path.
Once you've confirmed a Mobilizer, your job shifts. You're not selling to them anymore — you're selling through them. Give them the ammunition to make the case internally. A champion who doesn't know how to position Mintlify to their VP Eng will lose the internal sale even if they want to win it.
The four things a DevRel champion needs to sell internally
1 — The business case in the VP Eng's language
DevRel champions describe Mintlify in DevRel language — better developer experience, cleaner docs, faster updates. Their VP Eng hears "nice to have." You need to help them reframe it in engineering throughput language before they walk into that conversation.
"When you talk to your VP Eng, lead with the engineering tax angle rather than the docs quality angle. The question that lands best at that level is: how much engineering time is your team spending closing the gap between what's shipped and what's documented? Coinbase had 20 minutes per update across a high-velocity team. That's the number that gets a VP's attention — not 'our docs will look better.'"
2 — The answer to "why not just hire a technical writer?"
The most common internal objection a DevRel champion will face. Arm them with the response before they walk into the conversation, not after they've already lost it.
"If your VP pushes back with 'why don't we just hire someone for this' — the answer is: hiring solves the content problem but not the workflow problem. A new hire still can't update docs automatically when code changes. The staleness is structural, not a headcount gap. HubSpot had the same instinct and ended up cutting engineering resources on docs by 50% by fixing the infrastructure instead. That's the reframe."
3 — The answer to "what does this cost and who's paying for it?"
Champions get ambushed by the budget question and go silent because they don't know how to handle it. Give them a frame before they need it.
"On budget — if this comes up before we've scoped it together, the framing I'd suggest is: this sits in the engineering infrastructure budget, not the DevRel discretionary budget. It's documentation infrastructure, not a tools subscription. The ROI conversation is about engineering hours recaptured, not feature comparison. That framing tends to land better at the VP level."
4 — The answer to "why now — we have other priorities"
Urgency is the hardest thing for a champion to manufacture internally. Connect the decision to something the VP Eng already cares about — don't make them invent the urgency themselves.
"The 'why now' that lands best is connecting it to whatever your VP Eng is already focused on. If there's an AI initiative: 'our current documentation infrastructure isn't machine-readable, which creates a dependency risk for the AI roadmap.' If there's a headcount freeze: 'we're being asked to do more with less; this recaptures engineering time we're currently spending on manual documentation maintenance.' Find the thing they're already worried about and show how this solves it."

A champion going quiet is not a rejection — it's information. The question is whether they went quiet because they're busy, because they hit an internal wall, or because they were a Talker all along. Your response depends entirely on which one it is.
Three reasons champions go quiet — diagnose before you respond
Reason 1 — They're genuinely busy (most common)
Recoverable

Signal: they were responsive before, the silence is recent, and there's an identifiable external reason — quarter end, a product launch, a reorg. This is a timing problem, not a champion problem.

Re-engagement:
"Hey [Name] — not trying to crowd your inbox during what I'm sure is a busy [Q-end / launch / whatever]. Saw [specific thing about their company] and wanted to share something that's directly relevant. [One-sentence insight or proof point]. No action needed — just thought it might be useful context for when you surface for air. Happy to pick back up when timing works."

The personalization signal and low-pressure close tells them this isn't a follow-up for the sake of following up. That's what gets a reply from a busy Mobilizer.

Reason 2 — They hit an internal wall
Recoverable with the right move

Signal: they were advancing the deal, had an internal conversation, and then went quiet. The conversation didn't go well and they don't know what to do next. They're avoiding you because they feel like they failed.

Give them a way back in:
"Hey [Name] — wanted to check in, not to push, just because I've been in situations where someone ran into friction internally and didn't know how to navigate it — and it's usually easier if we figure it out together rather than me waiting on the sidelines. Did the conversation with your VP not go the way you expected? If so, that's completely fine — I'd rather know so I can help you think about the next move."

Most champions who hit a wall go quiet because they're embarrassed — not because the deal is dead. Opening the door directly almost always gets a response.

Reason 3 — They were a Talker all along
Requires a different strategy

Signal: they were never consistently responsive, they never completed any of the test asks, and the silence isn't tied to a specific internal event — they just gradually disengaged. The deal was probably never real.

The honest close — one final email:
"Hey [Name] — I'll keep this short. I've been genuinely trying to figure out whether there's a path to making this work at [company], but I'm having trouble reading the signals. If the timing isn't right or the internal appetite isn't there, I'd honestly rather know — it saves us both time and I can revisit when things change. If there is still interest, the one thing that would move this forward is [specific ask — usually the EB intro]. Let me know either way and I'll take my cue from you."

This email converts at 40–50% even on cold leads. It works because it's honest, has a clear ask, and gives them an easy out. If they respond with the action — Mobilizer. If they respond with more vague enthusiasm — Talker confirmed.

After you send it — what to do in Notion:
A
Update Champion Strength → Talker. Stage → Nurture. Set 60-day revisit in Actions.
B
Start outbound directly to the VP Eng via LinkedIn. Do not let a Talker block you from the real champion inside the account.
The time management rule: Once a champion is confirmed Talker, you get one re-engagement attempt. If that doesn't move, reclassify and redirect your time. You have 16 deals to close this year. A comfortable Talker relationship is not pipeline.
