The 25-minute EB meeting structure
1
Open with the reframe, not an intro (0–3 min)
Do not start with "let me tell you about Mintlify." Start with the commercial insight — their documentation problem is a workflow infrastructure problem, not a content problem. Let them react. Their reaction tells you whether to go deeper on the engineering tax angle or the AI readiness angle.

3
The proof point — one story, matched to their world (8–13 min)
One proof point only. Matched to their vertical and primary pain. Deliver it as a story — the problem, what they tried, what actually worked, the result. Do not list features. The proof point's job is to make the outcome feel real and achievable, not to demonstrate product capability.
4
The delta — what changes with Mintlify (13–18 min)
Show the before/after. Use the ROI model numbers. Frame it as infrastructure, not tooling — they're not buying a documentation tool, they're fixing a workflow that has been creating a compounding tax on their engineering org. Migration objection: Laravel fully migrated in 3 days. Security objection: Fidelity, 99.99% uptime in regulated environment.

Anticipated objections — with responses
"We have bigger priorities right now"
Most common EB objection
This is a prioritization objection, not a "no." The EB is telling you the business case hasn't competed effectively with their existing priorities. Your job is to connect it to something they're already prioritizing — not argue that docs should be a higher priority.
"That makes sense — I'd never argue that documentation should be your top priority. The reason I'm here is that this usually isn't a new project, it's an underlying cost that's making your current priorities harder. If your team is building [AI initiative / new API / scaling engineering], the documentation workflow tax is a drag on that work right now, not a separate problem. The question is whether it's worth $X to remove that drag while you focus on the things that matter more."
"Why not just hire a technical writer?"
Common alternative
The hiring instinct is understandable — it's the obvious response to a content quality problem. The challenge is that this isn't a content quality problem. A technical writer cannot fix structural staleness.
"A senior technical writer runs $150–180k fully loaded, and they'd solve the coverage problem — but not the workflow problem. Your docs would still go stale the moment code ships, because the documentation workflow isn't integrated with the engineering workflow. HubSpot had the same instinct — hired writers, still had staleness, still had engineering overhead. Fixing the infrastructure cut their engineering resources on docs by 50% without adding headcount. That's the difference."
"Migration is too disruptive"
Risk objection
Migration risk is real and reasonable. The mistake is letting it become a veto rather than a scoped evaluation. Use the Laravel proof point and offer a phased approach.
"That's a legitimate concern — migration risk is real. What we've consistently seen is that companies overestimate it. Laravel fully migrated in 3 days. AT&T moved a legacy enterprise documentation system in a regulated environment without disruption. What we'd propose is starting with one product line or one team — not a full migration — so you can validate the workflow fit before committing. The risk is scoped, the signal is real."
"Can you pass our security review?"
IT / Compliance gate
Do not get caught off guard by this. Have the answer ready before they ask. If this comes up in the EB meeting, it means you're past the business case and into the evaluation — which is a good sign.
"Yes — and we've been through rigorous security reviews at regulated enterprises. Fidelity is a customer — they're running at 99.99% uptime since launch in a heavily regulated environment. We're SOC 2 Type II certified, we support SSO/SAML, and we have a standard security questionnaire response your InfoSec team can work through. I'd suggest we schedule a technical call with your InfoSec lead as the next step so we don't let this become a bottleneck. I'll send you the documentation package before that call."
"Your numbers seem high — we don't spend that much on docs"
Math challenge
This is actually a great objection — it means they're engaging with the model. Don't defend your numbers. Invite them to correct them.
"That's fair — these are estimates and I'd rather get to the real number. Walk me through what you think it looks like: how many engineers are touching documentation on a weekly basis, and how long does a typical update take? I'll update the model in real time and we can work from your numbers instead of mine. The point isn't to win an argument about the estimate — it's to find out if the number is big enough to warrant a solution."
Then open the ROI calculator and update it live in the meeting with their corrections. Their engagement with the numbers is the qualification signal.
"We're locked into Confluence through IT / procurement"
Inertia objection
IT consolidation is a real constraint at enterprise accounts. The Challenger reframe: Confluence was built for internal wikis. Developer-facing documentation and AI-native knowledge infrastructure is a different category.
"Totally understand — IT consolidation mandates are real and I'm not here to blow that up. The way most of our enterprise customers have navigated it is by positioning Mintlify as developer documentation infrastructure rather than a wiki replacement — which means it lives outside the Confluence mandate in the engineering tools budget. Confluence stays for internal wikis and project management. Mintlify handles external developer docs, API references, and the AI-readable knowledge layer. They don't compete. Does that framing change how you'd approach the procurement question?"
